% In this paper, we analyze the Casimir honeycomb drive through the lens of Noether’s theorem.
In this paper the Casimir honeycomb drive is analyzed with the help of Noether’s theorem.
The system under consideration - a perfectly conducting plate with a nanoscale honeycomb structure
on one side - lacks translational symmetry along the axis normal to the plate
due to the geometric asymmetry of vacuum modes on its sides.
As a consequence, the vacuum Hamiltonian depends explicitly
on the coordinate \(l\) (the effective cavity length), and by Noether’s theorem,
the corresponding component of momentum is not conserved.
This non-conservation manifests as a net quantum-vacuum force (Casimir thrust).
It is shown that the derived expression for the force per unit area directly reflects this broken symmetry
and is consistent with both Hamiltonian approach and the difference in the energy density.
The result underscores a profound physical principle: macroscopic thrust from quantum vacuum fluctuations
arises because the system is not invariant under spatial translations.

\vspace{5mm}

\textit{Noether’s theorem, Casimir effect, quantum vacuum, non-conservation of momentum, nanohoneycomb, Casimir thrust}
\end{keywords}

\vspace{\myvspacebeforesubsection}
\subsection*{\centering{\fontencoding{T1}\fontfamily{ptm}{\selectfont INTRODUCTION}}}
\vspace{\myvspaceaftersubsection}

Noether’s theorem establishes a profound correspondence between continuous symmetries of a physical
system and conserved values of quantities. In particular, invariance under spatial translations implies conservation of linear momentum.
In the classical two-plate Casimir configuration, the system remains symmetric under translations perpendicular to the plates,
so transverse momentum is conserved; only the normal component is affected by boundary conditions.

In contrast, the \textit{one-body} Casimir honeycomb drive breaks translational symmetry explicitly along the \(z\)-axis
due to different boundary conditions on the different sides of a single plate: one side being smooth
and another structured with conductive nanocavities. This asymmetry renders the vacuum Hamiltonian
\(\langle 0|\hat{\mathcal{H}}|0\rangle\) a function of the geometric parameter \(l\), which represents the effective
length of the nanocavities.

\vspace{\myvspacebeforesubsection}
\subsection*{\centering{\fontencoding{T1}\fontfamily{ptm}{\selectfont NOETHER’S THEOREM AND MOMENTUM NON-CONSERVATION}}}
\vspace{\myvspaceaftersubsection}

Let us consider the vacuum Hamiltonian per unit area derived for the honeycomb system:

\begin{figure}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{honeycomb_box_H.png}
\caption{}{\centering{\textbf{Fig.1.} Cubic cavity with a plate covered by honeycomb.}}
\end{center}
\label{fig:honeycomb_box_H}
\end{figure}

\end{multicols}

\[
\frac{\langle 0|\hat{\mathcal{H}}|0\rangle}{S} = \frac{\hbar c}{a^2\pi} \Bigg\{
l \sum_{n_x=(0)1}^{\infty}\sum_{n_y=(0)1}^{\infty}
\int_0^{\infty} \sqrt{ \frac{\pi^2}{a^2}(n_x^2 + n_y^2) + k_z^2 } \, dk_z
+ (L - l) \int_0^{\infty}\!\!\int_0^{\infty}
\int_0^{\infty} \sqrt{k_x^2 + k_y^2 + k_z^2} \, dk_z \, \frac{a}{\pi}dk_x \frac{a}{\pi}dk_y
\Bigg\}.
\]

\begin{multicols}{2}

This expression depends explicitly on \(l\). Hence, the system is \textbf{not invariant} under infinitesimal translations \(z \to z + \delta z\).

According to Noether’s theorem, the generator of spatial translations—momentum component \(P_z\) — is conserved
\textbf{if and only if} the Hamiltonian is independent of \(z\).
Since \(\partial \langle H \rangle / \partial l \neq 0\), the symmetry is broken,
and \(P_z\) is not conserved.

The resulting force is
\end{multicols}

\[
\frac{F}{S} = \frac{\partial}{\partial l} \frac{\langle 0|\hat{\mathcal{H}}|0\rangle}{S}
= \frac{\hbar c}{a^2\pi} \left\{
\sum_{n_x=(0)1}^{\infty}\sum_{n_y=(0)1}^{\infty}
\int_0^{\infty} \sqrt{ \frac{\pi^2}{a^2}(n_x^2 + n_y^2) + k_z^2 } \, dk_z
- \int_0^{\infty}\!\!\int_0^{\infty}
\int_0^{\infty} \sqrt{k_x^2 + k_y^2 + k_z^2} \, dk_z \, dn_x dn_y
\right\},
\]

\begin{multicols}{2}
which matches the Casimir thrust formula previously obtained via the difference in energy density.

Thus, the appearance of net thrust is not unexpected - it is the consequence of a broken translational symmetry.
The quantum vacuum “pushes” more strongly on one side because the quantun vacuum oscillation mode structure differs,
and Noether’s theorem provides the fundamental explanation why momentum does not need to be conserved.

\vspace{\myvspacebeforesubsection}
\subsection*{\centering{\fontencoding{T1}\fontfamily{ptm}{\selectfont ON THE INCOMPLETENESS OF THE STANDARD MOMENTUM BALANCE}}}
\vspace{\myvspaceaftersubsection}

It is essential to specify \emph{which} momentum is not conserved, so that the
result is not misread as a violation of a fundamental conservation law. The
standard momentum-balance equation of continuum electrodynamics (Tamm,
\S\,105) reads
\end{multicols}
\[
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{T} = \frac{\partial\mathbf{g}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{vol}},
\qquad
\mathbf{g} = \frac{1}{c^2}\mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{4\pi c}[\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}],
\]
\begin{multicols}{2}
where $\mathbf{T}$ is the Maxwell stress tensor, $\mathbf{g}$ the field
momentum density and $\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{vol}}$ the volume (ponderomotive)
force density. For the vacuum fluctuations of a static honeycomb system the
mean Poynting vector vanishes, $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = 0$, hence
$\langle\partial\mathbf{g}/\partial t\rangle = 0$.

 The customary identification
of the field momentum with $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{S}/c^2$ therefore misses the
contribution that remains: the \emph{surface} integral of the stress tensor
over the boundary of the vacuum region.

Consider a closed pillbox surface $\partial V_{\mathrm{vac}}$ whose faces lie in
the free vacuum at $z = \pm L/2$ on either side of the plate (the plate is
entirely enclosed, and the lateral faces cancel by in-plane symmetry). 


\begin{figure}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{honeycomb_box_L.png}
\caption{}{\centering{\textbf{Fig.2.} Cubic cavity with a plate covered by honeycomb.}}
\end{center}
\label{fig:honeycomb_box_L}
\end{figure}


The net
force is
\end{multicols}
\[
F_z = \oint_{\partial V_{\mathrm{vac}}} \langle T_{zz}\rangle \, da
= \big[\langle T_{zz}\rangle_{z=+L/2} - \langle T_{zz}\rangle_{z=-L/2}\big]\,S .
\]
\begin{multicols}{2}
Because the faces lie in the vacuum, this is the flux of the \emph{field}
momentum through a purely vacuum boundary, not a force applied directly to the
substance of the plate. The difference is non-zero solely because the mode
structure --- and hence $\langle T_{zz}\rangle$ --- differs on the honeycomb and
the smooth side. This is precisely the term omitted when the balance is reduced
to $\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{S}/c^2$ with $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle=0$.

Quantitatively, the surface integral reproduces the thrust obtained earlier from
the difference of specific energy densities \cite{Drozdov2024}: for a gold
nano-honeycomb with cell size $a\approx 2~\mu$m,
\[
\frac{F}{S} = \langle T_{zz}\rangle_{\mathrm{honey}} - \langle T_{zz}\rangle_{\mathrm{smooth}}
\approx 8.55~\text{dyn/m}^2 = 8.55\times10^{-5}~\text{N/m}^2 ,
\]
of the order $\langle T_{zz}\rangle \sim K\,\hbar c/a^4$ with a dimensionless
geometric coefficient $K\sim 4\times10^{-2}$ set by the cell mode structure.
Thus the \S\,105 reading does not re-derive the force but \emph{interprets} it:
the same number is the flux of field momentum through the vacuum boundary.

\textbf{Where the standard derivation is incomplete.}

The passage from volume forces to stresses rests on the Gauss theorem, which
Tamm applies (\S\,33, eq.~33.7) under the explicit condition that the
components of the stress tensor are \emph{continuous} inside the volume; surfaces
of discontinuity are excluded there, and only their surface \emph{charges} are
treated later (\S\,34, eq.~34.8). A vacuum--metal boundary of a resonator can carry
zero surface charge and still produce a finite jump of the stress tensor: the tensor
evaluated with the fields on the vacuum side differs from the one evaluated with the
fields inside the wall. On the two sides of such a surface element the stresses are
then \emph{not} equal and opposite. This case is not considered in \S\,33, nor
anywhere in Tamm's book, including \S\,105. For a volume $V$ that contains internal
discontinuity surfaces $S_j$ the Gauss theorem has to be written as
\end{multicols}
\[
\int_V \frac{\partial T_{ik}}{\partial x_k}\,dV
= \oint_{\partial V_{\mathrm{ext}}} T_{ik}\,n_k\,dS
\;-\; \sum_j \int_{S_j} [\![\,T_{ik}\,]\!]\,n_k\,dS_j ,
\]
\begin{multicols}{2}
where $[\![\cdot]\!]$ is the jump across $S_j$. The customary identification of the
field momentum with $\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{S}/c^2$ keeps only the external surface
integral and drops the jump terms.

\textbf{The jump on a honeycomb plate.}
The plate has two such internal boundaries: the bottoms of the cells on the
structured side and the smooth face on the other side. The stress on each of them is
the electromagnetic pressure of the vacuum modes. Appendix~B of \cite{Drozdov2024}
computes this pressure on the $xy$ face of an $a\times b\times h$ rectangular
resonator, $f_z/S=\int(H_x^2+H_y^2)\,dS/(8\pi S)$, mode by mode, and relates it
to the mode energy density $E/V$. For a wave propagating along $z$
\end{multicols}
\[
\frac{f_z/S}{E/V}=1 \quad\text{(electric mode, } H_z=0\text{)},
\qquad
\frac{f_z/S}{E/V}=\frac{a^2k_z^2}{a^2k_z^2+\pi^2(n_x^2+n_y^2)} \quad\text{(magnetic mode, } E_z=0\text{)},
\]
\begin{multicols}{2}
and for every mode type $\lim_{k_z\to\infty}(f_z/S)/(E/V)=1$. Summed over the
zero-point modes, the jump of $\langle T_{zz}\rangle$ across each boundary is
therefore fixed by the vacuum energy density of the adjacent cavity, and the
difference of the two jumps gives
\[
\frac{F}{S}=[\![\langle T_{zz}\rangle]\!]_{\mathrm{honey}}-[\![\langle T_{zz}\rangle]\!]_{\mathrm{smooth}}
\;\ge\;\delta\frac{E}{V}=\hbar c\,\pi\frac{R}{a^4},
\]
the inequality of \cite{Drozdov2024} (Appendix~B). It coincides with the
Hamiltonian result $\partial\langle H\rangle/\partial l$ of the previous section.
For a gold nano-honeycomb with cell size $a\approx 2~\mu$m this gives
$F/S\approx 8.55~\text{dyn/m}^2 = 8.55\times10^{-5}~\text{N/m}^2$.
The \S\,105 reading, completed by the jump terms, therefore does not re-derive the
force but \emph{locates} it: the unbalanced momentum sits in the jump of the vacuum
stress tensor on the internal boundaries, which the reduction to
$\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{S}/c^2$ drops.

\textbf{Equivalent pillbox form.}
Enclosing the whole plate by a pillbox $\partial V_{\mathrm{vac}}$ with faces in the
free vacuum at $z=\pm d$ (lateral faces cancel by in-plane symmetry) turns the sum of
the jumps into an external flux,
$F_z=\oint_{\partial V_{\mathrm{vac}}}\langle T_{zz}\rangle\,da
=\big[\langle T_{zz}\rangle_{z=+d}-\langle T_{zz}\rangle_{z=-d}\big]S$.
This is the flux of \emph{field} momentum through a purely vacuum boundary, non-zero
solely because the mode structure, and hence $\langle T_{zz}\rangle$, differs on the
honeycomb and on the smooth side.

Two remarks make the interpretation unambiguous.
First, it is the momentum of the \emph{field} (equivalently, of the material plate that
absorbs it), not the total momentum of the closed laboratory system, that is not
conserved; the total momentum of plate, field and support is conserved, exactly as for
an ordinary sail in a wind. Second, the renormalised vacuum stress
$\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle$ is a physically measured quantity --- the Casimir effect
itself --- so the jump terms require no assumption of a material aether: their value
is fixed by the same $\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle$ that is confirmed experimentally for
the symmetric configuration. The broken translational symmetry of the boundary
conditions, not of space itself, is what renders $\partial\langle H\rangle/\partial l\neq 0$.


\vspace{\myvspacebeforesubsection}
\subsection*{\centering{\fontencoding{T1}\fontfamily{ptm}{\selectfont CONCLUSION}}}
\vspace{\myvspaceaftersubsection}

The Casimir honeycomb drive exemplifies a physical system where Noether’s theorem directly
explains the origin of macroscopic force: \textbf{the lack of spatial homogeneity leads
to non-conservation of momentum}, and hence to the appearance of thrust. This demonstrates that quantum
vacuum effects are not mere mathematical artifacts but manifestations of deep symmetry principles.
Experimental verification can not only confirm the reality of virtual photons but also validate
the link between geometry, symmetry, and dynamics in quantum field theory.

\end{multicols}

У цій роботі аналізується стильниковий рушій Казимира через призму теореми Нетер.
Розглянута система — ідеально провідна пластина з нанорозмірною сотовою структурою
з одного боку — не має трансляційної симетрії вздовж осі, нормальної до пластини,
через геометричну асиметрію вакуумних мод з двох її сторін. Як наслідок,
гамільтоніан вакууму явно залежить від координати \(l\) (ефективної довжини резонатора),
і, згідно з теоремою Нетер, відповідна компонента імпульсу не зберігається.
Це не збереження проявляється як результуюча сила квантового вакууму (тяга Казимира).
Показано, що отриманий вираз для сили на одиницю площі безпосередньо відображає цю порушену
симетрію й узгоджується як з гамільтоновим підходом, так і з різницею густин енергії.
Результат підкреслює глибокий фізичний принцип: макроскопічна тяга від квантових флуктуацій
вакууму виникає саме тому, що система не є інваріантною відносно просторових зсувів.
Окремо показано, що стандартне застосування рівняння балансу імпульсу (\S\,105 Тамма)
є неповним для систем із $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle=0$: оскільки середній вектор Пойнтінга
дорівнює нулю, незбалансований імпульс міститься у поверхневому інтегралі тензора натягів
Максвелла по межі вакуумної області, а не у потоці $\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{S}/c^2$. Не
зберігається імпульс \emph{поля} (речовини), а не повний імпульс замкненої системи;
величина сили спирається на виміряний вакуумний тензор $\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle$ і не
потребує припущення про матеріальний ефір.
