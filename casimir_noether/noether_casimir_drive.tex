% In this paper, we analyze the Casimir honeycomb drive through the lens of Noether’s theorem.
In this paper the Casimir honeycomb drive is analyzed with the help of Noether’s theorem.
The system under consideration - a perfectly conducting plate with a nanoscale honeycomb structure
on one side - lacks translational symmetry along the axis normal to the plate
due to the geometric asymmetry of vacuum modes on its sides.
As a consequence, the vacuum Hamiltonian depends explicitly
on the coordinate \(l\) (the effective cavity length), and by Noether’s theorem,
the corresponding component of momentum is not conserved.
This non-conservation manifests as a net quantum-vacuum force (Casimir thrust).
It is shown that the derived expression for the force per unit area directly reflects this broken symmetry
and is consistent with both Hamiltonian approach and the difference in the energy density.
The result underscores a profound physical principle: macroscopic thrust from quantum vacuum fluctuations
arises because the system is not invariant under spatial translations.

\vspace{5mm}

\noindent
\begin{keywords}
\textit{Noether’s theorem, Casimir effect, quantum vacuum, non-conservation of momentum, nanohoneycomb, Casimir thrust}
\end{keywords}

\vspace{\myvspacebeforesubsection}
\subsection*{\centering{\fontencoding{T1}\fontfamily{ptm}{\selectfont INTRODUCTION}}}
\vspace{\myvspaceaftersubsection}

Noether’s theorem establishes a profound correspondence between continuous symmetries of a physical
system and conserved values of quantities. In particular, invariance under spatial translations implies conservation of linear momentum.
In the classical two-plate Casimir configuration, the system remains symmetric under translations perpendicular to the plates,
so transverse momentum is conserved; only the normal component is affected by boundary conditions.

In contrast, the \textit{one-body} Casimir honeycomb drive breaks translational symmetry explicitly along the \(z\)-axis
due to different boundary conditions on the different sides of a single plate: one side being smooth
and another structured with conductive nanocavities. This asymmetry renders the vacuum Hamiltonian
\(\langle 0|\hat{\mathcal{H}}|0\rangle\) a function of the geometric parameter \(l\), which represents the effective
length of the nanocavities.

\vspace{\myvspacebeforesubsection}
\subsection*{\centering{\fontencoding{T1}\fontfamily{ptm}{\selectfont NOETHER’S THEOREM AND MOMENTUM NON-CONSERVATION}}}
\vspace{\myvspaceaftersubsection}

Let us consider the vacuum Hamiltonian per unit area derived for the honeycomb system:

\begin{figure}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{honeycomb_box_H.png}
\caption{}{\centering{\textbf{Fig.1.} Cubic cavity with a plate covered by honeycomb.}}
\end{center}
\label{fig:honeycomb_box_H}
\end{figure}

\end{multicols}

\[
\frac{\langle 0|\hat{\mathcal{H}}|0\rangle}{S} = \frac{\hbar c}{a^2\pi} \Bigg\{
l \sum_{n_x=(0)1}^{\infty}\sum_{n_y=(0)1}^{\infty}
\int_0^{\infty} \sqrt{ \frac{\pi^2}{a^2}(n_x^2 + n_y^2) + k_z^2 } \, dk_z
+ (L - l) \int_0^{\infty}\!\!\int_0^{\infty}
\int_0^{\infty} \sqrt{k_x^2 + k_y^2 + k_z^2} \, dk_z \, \frac{a}{\pi}dk_x \frac{a}{\pi}dk_y
\Bigg\}.
\]

\begin{multicols}{2}

This expression depends explicitly on \(l\). Hence, the system is \textbf{not invariant} under infinitesimal translations \(z \to z + \delta z\).

According to Noether’s theorem, the generator of spatial translations—momentum component \(P_z\) — is conserved
\textbf{if and only if} the Hamiltonian is independent of \(z\).
Since \(\partial \langle H \rangle / \partial l \neq 0\), the symmetry is broken,
and \(P_z\) is not conserved.

The resulting force is
\end{multicols}

\[
\frac{F}{S} = \frac{\partial}{\partial l} \frac{\langle 0|\hat{\mathcal{H}}|0\rangle}{S}
= \frac{\hbar c}{a^2\pi} \left\{
\sum_{n_x=(0)1}^{\infty}\sum_{n_y=(0)1}^{\infty}
\int_0^{\infty} \sqrt{ \frac{\pi^2}{a^2}(n_x^2 + n_y^2) + k_z^2 } \, dk_z
- \int_0^{\infty}\!\!\int_0^{\infty}
\int_0^{\infty} \sqrt{k_x^2 + k_y^2 + k_z^2} \, dk_z \, dn_x dn_y
\right\},
\]

\begin{multicols}{2}
which matches the Casimir thrust formula previously obtained via the difference in energy density.

Thus, the appearance of net thrust is not unexpected - it is the consequence of a broken translational symmetry.
The quantum vacuum “pushes” more strongly on one side because the quantun vacuum oscillation mode structure differs,
and Noether’s theorem provides the fundamental explanation why momentum does not need to be conserved.



\vspace{\myvspacebeforesubsection}
\subsection*{\centering{\fontencoding{T1}\fontfamily{ptm}{\selectfont THE STRESS TENSOR FROM THE CAVITY MODE FIELDS: SURFACE INTEGRALS AND THE JUMP AT THE METAL}}}
\vspace{\myvspaceaftersubsection}

In this section the Maxwell stress tensor is computed directly from the fields of
the cavity eigenmodes, without assuming the momentum flux of a plane wave.
Gaussian units and Tamm's notation are used.

\textbf{1. Geometry.} A plate of zero thickness lies in the plane $z=0$ inside a cube
$L^3$ with perfectly conducting walls. On the side $z>0$ the plate carries perfectly
conducting cells of cross-section $a\times a$ whose walls reach the cube face $z=l$
(in \cite{Drozdov2024} $l=L/2$). On the side $z<0$ there is the smooth face of the
plate and the large cavity $L\times L\times(L-l)$. Each cavity is a rectangular
resonator $a\times b\times h$: for a cell $a=b$, $h=l$; for the large cavity
$a=b=L$, $h=L-l$. In the formulas below $z$ is measured from the resonator face
that coincides with the plate (for the large cavity this is $-z$).

\textbf{2. Mode fields.} Let $k_x=\pi n_x/a$, $k_y=\pi n_y/b$, $k_z=\pi n_z/h$,
$K^2=k_x^2+k_y^2$, $k^2=K^2+k_z^2$, $\omega=ck$. TM mode ($H_z=0$,
$n_x,n_y\ge1$, $n_z\ge0$):
\end{multicols}
\[
\begin{aligned}
E_z&=A\sin k_xx\,\sin k_yy\,\cos k_zz\,\cos\omega t, &
H_z&=0,\\
E_x&=-A\frac{k_xk_z}{K^2}\cos k_xx\,\sin k_yy\,\sin k_zz\,\cos\omega t, &
H_x&=-A\frac{k\,k_y}{K^2}\sin k_xx\,\cos k_yy\,\cos k_zz\,\sin\omega t,\\
E_y&=-A\frac{k_yk_z}{K^2}\sin k_xx\,\cos k_yy\,\sin k_zz\,\cos\omega t, &
H_y&=A\frac{k\,k_x}{K^2}\cos k_xx\,\sin k_yy\,\cos k_zz\,\sin\omega t.
\end{aligned}
\]
TE mode ($E_z=0$, $n_z\ge1$, $(n_x,n_y)\ne(0,0)$):
\[
\begin{aligned}
H_z&=A\cos k_xx\,\cos k_yy\,\sin k_zz\,\cos\omega t, &
E_z&=0,\\
H_x&=-A\frac{k_xk_z}{K^2}\sin k_xx\,\cos k_yy\,\cos k_zz\,\cos\omega t, &
E_x&=-A\frac{k\,k_y}{K^2}\cos k_xx\,\sin k_yy\,\sin k_zz\,\sin\omega t,\\
H_y&=-A\frac{k_yk_z}{K^2}\cos k_xx\,\sin k_yy\,\cos k_zz\,\cos\omega t, &
E_y&=A\frac{k\,k_x}{K^2}\sin k_xx\,\cos k_yy\,\sin k_zz\,\sin\omega t.
\end{aligned}
\]
\begin{multicols}{2}

\textbf{3. Check of the fields.} By substitution,
$\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\nabla\cdot\mathbf H=0$,
$\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac1c\partial_t\mathbf H$,
$\nabla\times\mathbf H=\frac1c\partial_t\mathbf E$ for $\omega=ck$, and the
perfect-conductor boundary conditions hold: tangential $\mathbf E$ and normal
$\mathbf H$ vanish on all six faces (at $x=0,a$: $E_y=E_z=0$, $H_x=0$; at $y=0,b$:
$E_x=E_z=0$, $H_y=0$; at $z=0,h$: $E_x=E_y=0$, $H_z=0$). These relations were also
checked numerically for the modes $(2,1,3)$ with $a:b:h=1:1.3:2.7$ (residual of
order $10^{-9}$).

\textbf{4. The tensor component.} Following Tamm,
$T_{ik}=\frac1{4\pi}(E_iE_k+H_iH_k)-\frac{\delta_{ik}}{8\pi}(E^2+H^2)$, and the force on
the bodies inside a closed surface is $\oint T_{ik}n_k\,dS$ (outward normal). Hence
\[
T_{zz}=\frac1{8\pi}\left(E_z^2+H_z^2-E_x^2-E_y^2-H_x^2-H_y^2\right).
\]
The momentum flux is $\Pi_{ik}=-T_{ik}$; the pressure on a horizontal element is
denoted $p\equiv-\langle T_{zz}\rangle$.

\textbf{5. Averaging over the cross-section and the period.} We average over the area
$S=ab$ and over the period $2\pi/\omega$. For $n_x,n_y\ge1$ each factor $\sin^2$ or
$\cos^2$ in $x$, in $y$ and in $t$ gives $\tfrac12$. For the TM mode:
\end{multicols}
\[
\langle E_z^2\rangle=\frac{A^2}{8}\cos^2k_zz,\qquad
\langle E_x^2+E_y^2\rangle=\frac{A^2}{8}\frac{k_x^2+k_y^2}{K^4}k_z^2\sin^2k_zz=\frac{A^2}{8}\frac{k_z^2}{K^2}\sin^2k_zz,\qquad
\langle H_x^2+H_y^2\rangle=\frac{A^2}{8}\frac{k^2}{K^2}\cos^2k_zz .
\]
\[
8\pi\langle T_{zz}\rangle=\frac{A^2}{8}\left[\cos^2k_zz\left(1-\frac{k^2}{K^2}\right)-\frac{k_z^2}{K^2}\sin^2k_zz\right]
=-\frac{A^2}{8}\frac{k_z^2}{K^2}\left(\cos^2k_zz+\sin^2k_zz\right)=-\frac{A^2}{8}\frac{k_z^2}{K^2},
\]
\begin{multicols}{2}
\noindent where $1-k^2/K^2=-k_z^2/K^2$ was used. The energy density
$w=(E^2+H^2)/8\pi$ of the same mode is
$8\pi\langle w\rangle(z)=\frac{A^2}{8}\left[\cos^2k_zz+\frac{k_z^2}{K^2}\sin^2k_zz+\frac{k^2}{K^2}\cos^2k_zz\right]$;
averaging also over $z$ (for $n_z\ge1$) gives
$8\pi\overline w=\frac{A^2}{16}\cdot\frac{K^2+k_z^2+k^2}{K^2}=\frac{A^2}{8}\frac{k^2}{K^2}$.
Therefore
\[
p=-\langle T_{zz}\rangle=\overline w\,\frac{k_z^2}{k^2}.
\]
For the TE mode the same steps give
$\langle H_z^2\rangle=\frac{A^2}{8}\sin^2k_zz$,
$\langle H_x^2+H_y^2\rangle=\frac{A^2}{8}\frac{k_z^2}{K^2}\cos^2k_zz$,
$\langle E_x^2+E_y^2\rangle=\frac{A^2}{8}\frac{k^2}{K^2}\sin^2k_zz$, hence
$8\pi\langle T_{zz}\rangle=-\frac{A^2}{8}\frac{k_z^2}{K^2}$ and the same result
$p=\overline w\,k_z^2/k^2$. If one transverse index is zero (possible only for TE),
the corresponding $\cos^2$ equals $1$ instead of $\tfrac12$; the common factor doubles
both in the pressure and in the energy, and the ratio is unchanged. The TM mode
with $n_z=0$ has $k_z=0$ and exerts no pressure.

\textbf{6. Independence of $z$.} In item~5 the $z$-dependence dropped out
($\cos^2+\sin^2=1$). The general reason: in vacuum
$\partial T_{zk}/\partial x_k=\frac1{4\pi c}\,\partial_t[\mathbf E\times\mathbf H]_z$,
and the period average of a time derivative vanishes. Integrating over the
cross-section leaves the fluxes through the side walls:
$T_{zx}=\frac1{4\pi}(E_zE_x+H_zH_x)$, and on the walls $x=0,a$ we have $E_z=0$
(tangential field) and $H_x=0$ (normal field), so $T_{zx}=0$; likewise for $T_{zy}$
at $y=0,b$. Hence $\frac{d}{dz}\int\langle T_{zz}\rangle\,dx\,dy=0$. Note that only the
cross-section integral is independent of $z$; at a given point $(x,y)$, $T_{zz}$ does
vary with $z$. The force involves the integral.

\textbf{7. Zero-point oscillations.} Each mode is an oscillator; in the ground state
its energy is $\hbar\omega/2$, with equal electric and magnetic parts
$\hbar\omega/4$. This is exactly the distribution given by the classical period
average of item~5 if the amplitude is chosen so that $\overline w\,V=\hbar\omega/2$.
The contribution of a mode to the pressure is therefore
$p_{\text{mode}}=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2V}\frac{k_z^2}{k^2}$, and the pressure in a cavity is
\[
p=\frac{\hbar c}{2V}\sum_{\text{modes}}\frac{k_z^2}{k},
\]
the sum running over TM and TE modes with the index ranges of item~2. It differs
from the energy sum, eq.~(1) of \cite{Drozdov2024}, only by the summand: $k_z^2/k$
instead of $k$.

\textit{Identity with the Hamiltonian approach.} For $k_z=\pi n_z/h$,
$\partial k/\partial h=-k_z^2/(kh)$, and for each mode
\[
-\frac{\partial}{\partial h}\frac{\hbar ck}{2}=\frac{\hbar c}{2h}\frac{k_z^2}{k}=S\,p_{\text{mode}},\qquad V=Sh .
\]
The force obtained from the stress tensor coincides with $-\partial E/\partial h$
mode by mode, before any regularization.

\textbf{8. Surfaces $z=\mathrm{const}$ for the honeycomb plate.} From items~5--7:
\begin{itemize}
\item $z\to0^+$ (vacuum at the cell bottom), $z=d$ with $0<d<l$, $z\to l^-$ (at the
auxiliary cube wall): $-\langle T_{zz}\rangle=p_{\text{cell}}=\frac{\hbar c}{2a^2l}\sum_{\text{cell}}\frac{k_z^2}{k}$;
\item $z\to0^-$ (vacuum at the smooth face), $z=-d'$ with $0<d'<L-l$, $z\to-(L-l)^+$:
$-\langle T_{zz}\rangle=p_{\text{big}}=\frac{\hbar c}{2L^2(L-l)}\sum_{\text{large}}\frac{k_z^2}{k}$;
\item inside the metal of the plate and of the walls: $T_{ik}=0$.
\end{itemize}
The plane $z=d$ in the cell region crosses the cell walls; for walls of zero
thickness they do not contribute to the integral, and the cells cover the whole
plate area.

\textbf{9. The jump at the metal surface.} At the face $z=0$ (vacuum outside, metal
inside with zero fields) the boundary conditions require continuity of tangential
$\mathbf E$ and normal $\mathbf H$; on the vacuum side they vanish: $E_x=E_y=0$,
$H_z=0$. Normal $\mathbf E$ and tangential $\mathbf H$ jump by $4\pi\sigma$ and
$\frac{4\pi}{c}\mathbf i$ respectively and are non-zero on the vacuum side. Hence
\[
T_{zz}\big|_{\text{vacuum}}=\frac1{8\pi}\left(E_z^2-H_x^2-H_y^2\right),\qquad
T_{zz}\big|_{\text{metal}}=0 .
\]
Explicitly, for the TM mode at $z=0$: $\langle E_z^2\rangle=\frac{A^2}{8}$,
$\langle H_x^2+H_y^2\rangle=\frac{A^2}{8}\frac{k^2}{K^2}$, so
$-\langle T_{zz}\rangle(0^+)=\frac{A^2}{64\pi}\left(\frac{k^2}{K^2}-1\right)=\frac{A^2}{64\pi}\frac{k_z^2}{K^2}=\overline w\,\frac{k_z^2}{k^2}$,
the same value as in the bulk of the cavity (item~6). For the TE mode at $z=0$,
$E_z=0$ and $-\langle T_{zz}\rangle(0^+)=\frac1{8\pi}\langle H_x^2+H_y^2\rangle=\overline w\,k_z^2/k^2$.
The jump $[\![T_{zz}]\!]=T_{zz}|_{\text{metal}}-T_{zz}|_{\text{vacuum}}=p$ is the surface
force per unit area acting on the surface charges $\sigma=E_n/4\pi$ in the field
$E_n$ and on the surface currents $\mathbf i=\frac{c}{4\pi}[\mathbf n\times\mathbf H]$ in
the field $\mathbf H_t$:
\[
\frac{f_z}{S}=\frac1{8\pi}\left(\langle H_t^2\rangle-\langle E_n^2\rangle\right).
\]
In the vacuum state $\langle\sigma\rangle=0$ and $\langle\mathbf i\rangle=0$, but the
correlations $\langle\sigma E_n\rangle$ and $\langle\mathbf i\times\mathbf H\rangle$ do not
vanish: the force is quadratic in the fields. For TE modes there is no surface
charge at all on the $xy$ faces ($E_n=E_z=0$), and the tensor jump is produced by the
surface currents alone. This is an explicit example of the case pointed out in the
remark on Tamm's \S\,33: a jump of the stress tensor at a surface that carries no
charge. For TM modes the fluctuating charges reduce the pressure by
$\langle E_n^2\rangle/8\pi$.

\textbf{10. The force on the plate.} Take a closed surface made of the faces $z=z_1$
($0<z_1\le l$) and $z=-z_2$ ($0<z_2\le L-l$) spanning the whole cross-section
$L\times L$; its lateral faces lie on the cube walls, where $T_{zx}=T_{zy}=0$
(item~6). Then
\[
F_z=\left[\langle T_{zz}\rangle(z_1)-\langle T_{zz}\rangle(-z_2)\right]L^2=(p_{\text{big}}-p_{\text{cell}})\,L^2,
\]
independent of $z_1$, $z_2$. For $z_1,z_2\to0$ this is the sum of the two jumps at
the faces of the plate (the force is applied to the metal surfaces); for $z_1=l$,
$z_2=L-l$ these are the faces at the auxiliary cube walls (for $l=L/2$, the faces
$z=\pm L/2$ above); intermediate positions give the same. $F_z>0$ (a push towards
the cells) when $p_{\text{big}}>p_{\text{cell}}$.

\textbf{11. Check on Casimir's two plates.} Plates at $z=0$ and $z=a$, outer cavities
up to $z=\pm L/2$. In the gap $k_z=\pi n/a$ and the transverse spectrum is
continuous; per unit area (two polarizations)
\[
p_{\text{in}}=\frac{\hbar c}{a}\sum_{n\ge1}\int\frac{d^2k_\parallel}{(2\pi)^2}\frac{k_z^2}{k}
\]
(the $n=0$ mode has $k_z=0$ and gives no pressure), and outside the sum over $n$ is
replaced by $\frac a\pi\int dk_z$. By item~6, $p_{\text{in}}$ is the same at the centre
of the gap and at the inner surface of each plate, and $p_{\text{out}}$ is the same at
the outer surface of each plate and at the faces $z=\pm L/2$ (for $L\to\infty$ it is
the free-vacuum value). With the cutoff $e^{-k/k_m}$ the difference
$p_{\text{in}}-p_{\text{out}}$ equals $-0.041043$, $-0.041110$, $-0.041120$ in units of
$\hbar c/a^4$ for $k_ma=20$, $50$, $100$ and tends to $-\pi^2/240=-0.041123$. The sign
means attraction; with the Casimir energy density $u=-\pi^2\hbar c/720a^4$ this is
$p=3u$ \cite{BrownMaclay1969}.

\textbf{12. Limitations and self-critical remarks.}

(a) \textit{Appendix B of \cite{Drozdov2024}.} There the pressure on the $xy$ face was
computed as $\langle H_x^2+H_y^2\rangle/8\pi$. For electric (TM) modes the term
$-\langle E_z^2\rangle/8\pi$ is missing. With it the ratio of pressure to energy
density is $k_z^2/k^2$ for both mode types, not $1$ for the electric mode (numerically
for the mode $(2,1,3)$: $0.2119$ instead of $1.0000$). Therefore the value $1$ for the
electric mode, and the inequality $F/S\ge\delta(E/V)$ based on it, in the section
``On the incompleteness of the standard momentum balance'' must be revised. The
Hamiltonian derivation of Appendix C is not affected: by item~7 it coincides with
the tensor result mode by mode.

(b) \textit{Regularization.} With a cutoff $f(k/k_m)$ the derivative of the mode
energy is
$-\partial_h\!\left[\frac{\hbar ck}{2}f\right]=\frac{\hbar c}{2h}\frac{k_z^2}{k}\left[f+\frac{k}{k_m}f'\right]$,
whereas the regularized tensor sum $\sum(k_z^2/k)f$ lacks the second term. For two
plates the difference disappears as $k_ma\to\infty$ (item~11). For the honeycomb at
finite $ak_m$ this has not been computed; this term may be related to the
dependence $R(ak_m)$. Which of the two regularized quantities is physical cannot be
decided within the perfect-conductor model with a mode cutoff; a real
$\varepsilon(\omega)$, as in \cite{DLP1961}, is needed.

(c) \textit{Cell height.} The calculation assumes that the cell walls reach the cube
face, so that each cell is a closed resonator. For real cells of height $h\ll L$ the
cell is open into the large cavity, the modes do not separate, and this calculation
does not describe that case.

(d) \textit{Perfect conductor.} The jump is a property of the perfect conductor. In a
real metal $T_{zz}$ decays to zero over the skin depth, and the integral of the force
over the layer equals the jump as long as the current in the metal is consistent
with the field: $\mathbf j=\frac c{4\pi}\nabla\times\mathbf H$.

(e) \textit{Wall thickness.} Cell walls of finite thickness carry no $z$-force
($T_{zx}=0$ on them) but reduce the fraction of the plate area occupied by the
cavities.

(f) \textit{Quantum average.} The equality of electric and magnetic parts of the mode
energy in the ground state (item~7) is used as a standard oscillator result and is
not derived here.

(g) \textit{What has been checked.} The fields and averages were checked numerically
for individual modes, and the limiting value for two plates. The sum $\sum k_z^2/k$
for the honeycomb plate, regularized with the Euler--Maclaurin formula of
\cite{Drozdov2024}, has not yet been evaluated.

\vspace{\myvspacebeforesubsection}
\subsection*{\centering{\fontencoding{T1}\fontfamily{ptm}{\selectfont THE STRESS TENSOR FROM THE ZERO-POINT MODE SUM}}}
\vspace{\myvspaceaftersubsection}

In \cite{Drozdov2024} the force was obtained from the zero-point energy
$\frac12\sum\hbar\omega$ evaluated with the two-dimensional Euler--Maclaurin
formula; the Maxwell stress tensor itself was not derived there. It follows from
the same mode sum.

\textbf{One mode.} For a plane wave with wave vector $\mathbf{k}$ and energy
density $u$ the momentum flux is $\Pi_{ij}=u\,k_ik_j/k^2$ (Tamm's tension is
$T_{ij}=-\Pi_{ij}$). A standing wave is the sum of the waves with $\pm k_z$ and has
the same $\Pi_{zz}$. A zero-point mode in a cavity of volume $V$ has
$u=\hbar\omega/2V$, hence
\[
\langle\Pi_{zz}\rangle=\frac{\hbar c}{2V}\sum_{n_x,n_y,n_z}\frac{k_z^2}{k}.
\]
This differs from the energy sum, eq.~(1) of \cite{Drozdov2024},
$E = \frac{1}{2}\sum\,\hbar\omega = \hbar\,c\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{n_x}^{}\sum\limits_{n_y}^{}\sum\limits_{n_z}^{}k$,
only by the
summand: $k_z^2/k$ instead of $k$.

\textbf{Link to the Hamiltonian.} With $k_z=\pi n_z/l$ one has
$\partial k/\partial l=-k_z^2/(k\,l)$, so for every mode separately
\[
-\frac{\partial}{\partial l}\,\frac{\hbar c\,k}{2}=\frac{\hbar c}{2\,l}\,\frac{k_z^2}{k}
= S\,\Pi_{zz}^{\mathrm{(mode)}},\qquad V=S\,l .
\]
Thus $-\partial E/\partial l=S\,\Pi_{zz}$ holds mode by mode (the Hellmann--Feynman
theorem \cite{Feynman1939}; equivalently, conservation of the adiabatic invariant
$E/\omega$ of each mode under a slow displacement of the wall). The Hamiltonian
force $\partial\langle H\rangle/\partial l$ of the preceding sections and the
stress-tensor force therefore coincide already before regularization.

\textbf{Regularization.} The cutoff function $f(k/k_m)$ and the Euler--Maclaurin
procedure of \cite{Drozdov2024} apply unchanged, with the function $G$ built from
$k_z^2/k$. At finite $k_m$ the two expressions may differ by terms containing
$f'(k/k_m)$, because $f$ depends on $|\mathbf{k}|$. Whether these terms are the
origin of the material dependence $R(ak_m)$ remains to be checked by calculation.

\textbf{Symmetry check.} The renormalized $\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle$ of the
electromagnetic field is traceless. Between two parallel plates the boundary
conditions are invariant under boosts along the plates; together with the trace
condition this gives $\langle\Pi_{zz}\rangle=3\langle u\rangle$ (Brown and Maclay
\cite{BrownMaclay1969}), which is the factor $F/S=-3\,\delta(E/V)$ found for
Casimir's configuration in \cite{Drozdov2024}. A honeycomb cell is a waveguide
along $z$: away from its ends its boundary conditions are invariant under boosts
along $z$, so in the $(t,z)$ block $\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle\propto\eta_{\mu\nu}$
and $\langle\Pi_{zz}\rangle=-\langle u\rangle$. This reproduces
$|F/S|=\delta(E/V)$ of Appendices~B and~C of \cite{Drozdov2024}. The argument is
exact for a perfect conductor in the limit $ak_m\to\infty$ and up to end
corrections of order $a/l$; the material cutoff breaks the boost invariance, and its
corrections require the mode sum above.

The same flux can be computed without a mode cutoff by the method of
Dzyaloshinskii, Lifshitz and Pitaevskii \cite{DLP1961}, who obtain the stress in a
vacuum gap from the Green function of the field, the material entering through
$\varepsilon(\omega)$. For a honeycomb cell this requires the Green function of a
rectangular waveguide and is left for future work.

\vspace{\myvspacebeforesubsection}
\subsection*{\centering{\fontencoding{T1}\fontfamily{ptm}{\selectfont ON THE AUXILIARY CAVITY AND THE POSITION OF THE PILLBOX}}}
\vspace{\myvspaceaftersubsection}

The perfectly conducting walls of the cube $L^3$ (Fig.~2) are, as in Casimir's
paper, an auxiliary boundary condition without a physical counterpart, in the same
way as the periodic boundary conditions used in textbooks of quantum mechanics to
normalize plane waves. The procedure is legitimate because the result is taken in
the limit $L\to\infty$. By Weyl's theorem \cite{Weyl1912} the leading term of the
number of modes, $N(k)\simeq Vk^3/3\pi^2$, does not depend on the boundary
conditions on the outer faces, which affect only surface corrections, and the
contribution of the remote walls to the force on the plate vanishes as
$L\to\infty$. The finite box makes the wave numbers discrete; it does not make the
space discrete. The cutoff $k_m$ is a separate, physical ingredient: it is set by
the transparency of the plate material for short waves. (If the vacuum were
regarded as a discrete medium with lattice step $b$, its Brillouin zone would add a
cutoff $|\mathbf{k}|\le\pi/b$; for the force on the plate, however, the relevant
cutoff is that of the plate material.)

\textbf{Position of the pillbox faces.} Take the faces at $z=\pm d$,
$0<d<L/2$, strictly inside the two cavities. There is no matter inside a cavity,
so $\partial_k\langle T_{zk}\rangle=0$. Integrated over the cross-section of a
cell, the flux through the side walls vanishes: on a perfect conductor $E_z=0$
(tangential) and $H_n=0$ (normal), so $T_{zn}=(E_zE_n+H_zH_n)/4\pi=0$. Hence
$\int\langle T_{zz}\rangle\,dx\,dy$ does not depend on $z$, and $F_z$ does not
depend on $d$. The faces at $z=\pm L/2$ used above are to be understood as the
limit $d\to L/2$ taken from inside the cavities; at $z=\pm L/2$ themselves stand
the auxiliary conducting walls. On the honeycomb side the face crosses the cell
walls; for perfectly conducting walls of zero thickness these do not contribute to
the integral.

\vspace{\myvspacebeforesubsection}
\subsection*{\centering{\fontencoding{T1}\fontfamily{ptm}{\selectfont ON THE INCOMPLETENESS OF THE STANDARD MOMENTUM BALANCE}}}
\vspace{\myvspaceaftersubsection}

It is essential to specify \emph{which} momentum is not conserved, so that the
result is not misread as a violation of a fundamental conservation law. The
standard momentum-balance equation of continuum electrodynamics (Tamm,
\S\,105) reads
\end{multicols}
\[
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{T} = \frac{\partial\mathbf{g}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{vol}},
\qquad
\mathbf{g} = \frac{1}{c^2}\mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{4\pi c}[\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}],
\]
\begin{multicols}{2}
where $\mathbf{T}$ is the Maxwell stress tensor, $\mathbf{g}$ the field
momentum density and $\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{vol}}$ the volume (ponderomotive)
force density. For the vacuum fluctuations of a static honeycomb system the
mean Poynting vector vanishes, $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = 0$, hence
$\langle\partial\mathbf{g}/\partial t\rangle = 0$.

 The customary identification
of the field momentum with $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{S}/c^2$ therefore misses the
contribution that remains: the \emph{surface} integral of the stress tensor
over the boundary of the vacuum region.

Consider a closed pillbox surface $\partial V_{\mathrm{vac}}$ whose faces lie in
the free vacuum at $z = \pm L/2$ on either side of the plate (the plate is
entirely enclosed, and the lateral faces cancel by in-plane symmetry). 


\begin{figure}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{honeycomb_box_L.png}
\caption{}{\centering{\textbf{Fig.2.} Cubic cavity with a plate covered by honeycomb.}}
\end{center}
\label{fig:honeycomb_box_L}
\end{figure}


The net
force is
\end{multicols}
\[
F_z = \oint_{\partial V_{\mathrm{vac}}} \langle T_{zz}\rangle \, da
= \big[\langle T_{zz}\rangle_{z=+L/2} - \langle T_{zz}\rangle_{z=-L/2}\big]\,S .
\]
\begin{multicols}{2}
Because the faces lie in the vacuum, this is the flux of the \emph{field}
momentum through a purely vacuum boundary, not a force applied directly to the
substance of the plate. The difference is non-zero solely because the mode
structure --- and hence $\langle T_{zz}\rangle$ --- differs on the honeycomb and
the smooth side. This is precisely the term omitted when the balance is reduced
to $\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{S}/c^2$ with $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle=0$.

Quantitatively, the surface integral reproduces the thrust obtained earlier from
the difference of specific energy densities \cite{Drozdov2024}: for a gold
nano-honeycomb with cell size $a\approx 2~\mu$m,
\[
\frac{F}{S} = \langle T_{zz}\rangle_{\mathrm{honey}} - \langle T_{zz}\rangle_{\mathrm{smooth}}
\approx 8.55~\text{dyn/m}^2 = 8.55\times10^{-5}~\text{N/m}^2 ,
\]
of the order $\langle T_{zz}\rangle \sim K\,\hbar c/a^4$ with a dimensionless
geometric coefficient $K\sim 4\times10^{-2}$ set by the cell mode structure.
Thus the \S\,105 reading does not re-derive the force but \emph{interprets} it:
the same number is the flux of field momentum through the vacuum boundary.





\textbf{Where the standard derivation is incomplete.}

The passage from volume forces to stresses rests on the Gauss theorem, which
Tamm applies (\S\,33, eq.~33.7) under the explicit condition that the
components of the stress tensor are \emph{continuous} inside the volume; surfaces
of discontinuity are excluded there, and only their surface \emph{charges} are
treated later (\S\,34, eq.~34.8). A vacuum--metal boundary of a resonator can carry
zero surface charge and still produce a finite jump of the stress tensor: the tensor
evaluated with the fields on the vacuum side differs from the one evaluated with the
fields inside the wall. On the two sides of such a surface element the stresses are
then \emph{not} equal and opposite. This case is not considered in \S\,33, nor
anywhere in Tamm's book, including \S\,105. For a volume $V$ that contains internal
discontinuity surfaces $S_j$ the Gauss theorem has to be written as
\end{multicols}
\[
\int_V \frac{\partial T_{ik}}{\partial x_k}\,dV
= \oint_{\partial V_{\mathrm{ext}}} T_{ik}\,n_k\,dS
\;-\; \sum_j \int_{S_j} [\![\,T_{ik}\,]\!]\,n_k\,dS_j ,
\]
\begin{multicols}{2}
where $[\![\cdot]\!]$ is the jump across $S_j$. The customary identification of the
field momentum with $\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{S}/c^2$ keeps only the external surface
integral and drops the jump terms.

\textbf{The jump on a honeycomb plate.}
The plate has two such internal boundaries: the bottoms of the cells on the
structured side and the smooth face on the other side. The stress on each of them is
the electromagnetic pressure of the vacuum modes. Appendix~B of \cite{Drozdov2024}
computes this pressure on the $xy$ face of an $a\times b\times h$ rectangular
resonator, $f_z/S=\int(H_x^2+H_y^2)\,dS/(8\pi S)$, mode by mode, and relates it
to the mode energy density $E/V$. For a wave propagating along $z$
\end{multicols}
\[
\frac{f_z/S}{E/V}=1 \quad\text{(electric mode, } H_z=0\text{)},
\qquad
\frac{f_z/S}{E/V}=\frac{a^2k_z^2}{a^2k_z^2+\pi^2(n_x^2+n_y^2)} \quad\text{(magnetic mode, } E_z=0\text{)},
\]
\begin{multicols}{2}
and for every mode type $\lim_{k_z\to\infty}(f_z/S)/(E/V)=1$. Summed over the
zero-point modes, the jump of $\langle T_{zz}\rangle$ across each boundary is
therefore fixed by the vacuum energy density of the adjacent cavity, and the
difference of the two jumps gives
\[
\frac{F}{S}=[\![\langle T_{zz}\rangle]\!]_{\mathrm{honey}}-[\![\langle T_{zz}\rangle]\!]_{\mathrm{smooth}}
\;\ge\;\delta\frac{E}{V}=\hbar c\,\pi\frac{R}{a^4},
\]
the inequality of \cite{Drozdov2024} (Appendix~B). It coincides with the
Hamiltonian result $\partial\langle H\rangle/\partial l$ of the previous section.
For a gold nano-honeycomb with cell size $a\approx 2~\mu$m this gives
$F/S\approx 8.55~\text{dyn/m}^2 = 8.55\times10^{-5}~\text{N/m}^2$.
The \S\,105 reading, completed by the jump terms, therefore does not re-derive the
force but \emph{locates} it: the unbalanced momentum sits in the jump of the vacuum
stress tensor on the internal boundaries, which the reduction to
$\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{S}/c^2$ drops.

\textbf{Equivalent pillbox form.}
Enclosing the whole plate by a pillbox $\partial V_{\mathrm{vac}}$ with faces in the
free vacuum at $z=\pm d$ (lateral faces cancel by in-plane symmetry) turns the sum of
the jumps into an external flux,
$F_z=\oint_{\partial V_{\mathrm{vac}}}\langle T_{zz}\rangle\,da
=\big[\langle T_{zz}\rangle_{z=+d}-\langle T_{zz}\rangle_{z=-d}\big]S$.
This is the flux of \emph{field} momentum through a purely vacuum boundary, non-zero
solely because the mode structure, and hence $\langle T_{zz}\rangle$, differs on the
honeycomb and on the smooth side.

Two remarks make the interpretation unambiguous.
First, it is the momentum of the \emph{field} (equivalently, of the material plate that
absorbs it), not the total momentum of the closed laboratory system, that is not
conserved; the total momentum of plate, field and support is conserved, exactly as for
an ordinary sail in a wind. Second, the renormalised vacuum stress
$\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle$ is a physically measured quantity --- the Casimir effect
itself --- so the jump terms require no assumption of a material aether: their value
is fixed by the same $\langle T_{\mu\nu}\rangle$ that is confirmed experimentally for
the symmetric configuration. The broken translational symmetry of the boundary
conditions, not of space itself, is what renders $\partial\langle H\rangle/\partial l\neq 0$.


\vspace{\myvspacebeforesubsection}
\subsection*{\centering{\fontencoding{T1}\fontfamily{ptm}{\selectfont CONCLUSION}}}
\vspace{\myvspaceaftersubsection}

The Casimir honeycomb drive exemplifies a physical system where Noether’s theorem directly
explains the origin of macroscopic force: \textbf{the lack of spatial homogeneity leads
to non-conservation of momentum}, and hence to the appearance of thrust. This demonstrates that quantum
vacuum effects are not mere mathematical artifacts but manifestations of deep symmetry principles.
Experimental verification can not only confirm the reality of virtual photons but also validate
the link between geometry, symmetry, and dynamics in quantum field theory.

\end{multicols}
